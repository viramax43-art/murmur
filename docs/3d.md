# 3D: what it would take, and what it would actually look like

The ask was "make the game 3D, 4K realism". This file is the honest answer: what
that costs, what this machine can do, which of the four readings of the request
is worth building — and what was built (variant **A**, in three passes: the beat
stage, the walkable world, and then the walkable world's cast rebuilt in volumes).

## Realism is assets, resolution is arithmetic

Four separate things get called "realism", and only one of them is a format
change:

| ingredient | what it is | cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pixels | a 3840×2160 render target | free-ish (8.29 Mpx per frame) |
| geometry | real 3D meshes instead of painted planes | modelling, or a base-mesh bundle |
| surfaces | PBR albedo/normal/roughness, ideally scanned, 2–4K each | the expensive part |
| light | physical transport, or a good approximation of it | engine work |
| lens | tonemapping, vignette, grain, DOF | shader work, cheap |

Switching the stage to 3D buys depth, perspective and real light on the walls.
It does not buy skin, cloth or faces. Those come from assets, and the assets the
project has are a 1536×1024 painted concept sheet and Blender's free human base
meshes.

## What the repo has today

- **Gameplay is 2D by construction.** The stage is a CSS box (`styles/main.css`
  `.stage`, 880×550, 16:10) holding absolutely-positioned `.obj` divs; beats
  move classes on those divs (`is-focus`, `is-speaking`, `is-tap`, `is-hidden`).
  Sprites are 12×12 string maps painted to canvas, or atlas crops. There is no
  matrix, camera or depth buffer anywhere in `src/`.
- **No dependencies, no build step.** `package.json` has no `dependencies`, no
  bundler; the browser loads ES modules straight from `src/`. A renderer is
  therefore either hand-written WebGL2 or a vendored three.js (~600 KB plus an
  update story).
- **A real 3D pipeline already exists** — `tools/blender/`: Cycles, Blender's
  official human base-mesh bundle, garment shells, 4200-strand hair, procedural
  skin/eye shaders. Two shots are staged and rendered:
  `assets/cutscenes/ch1-in.png`, `ch5-out.png`, both 1600×900.
- **The ceiling of that pipeline is visible in those PNGs.** Look at
  `assets/cutscenes/ch1-in.png`: correct blocking and one motivated light, but
  the people are soft, waxy CG — a 2000s cutscene, not a photograph. That is
  after full path tracing with denoising. Real-time will look *worse* than this
  still, because Cycles' global illumination becomes an approximation.

## Measured on this machine

- GPU: **Intel UHD Graphics** (0x9A68), WebGL2 through ANGLE/D3D11.
  `MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE` 16384, `MAX_SAMPLES` 16, float-linear filtering available.
  WebGL2 works even in headless Chrome, so the renderer can be screenshot-tested.
- 4K = 8.29 Mpx/frame. A forward PBR pass with one shadow map on integrated
  graphics lands around 10–20 fps at that size. **4K is a screenshot setting, not
  a playable one**; 1080p–1440p internal with upscale is the honest target.

## The four readings of "3D, 4K realism"

| | what changes | looks like | cost | risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. 3D room, 2D cast** | the stage becomes a WebGL2 scene: floor, walls, ceiling, window, props, sun with a shadow map, ACES tonemap, perspective camera with parallax. The cast stays the art pack's figures as depth-sorted billboards. | a puppet theatre inside a real room: the drawings keep their own style but finally cast shadows and sit in depth | one session | low — opt-in renderer, CSS fallback when WebGL2 is missing |
| **A+. 3D room, cast in volumes** | the same, but the walkable world's cast is rebuilt from primitives (`bodies3d.js`): a head, a chest, limbs, ears, a tail, walking and turning, in the card's own palette | a real body you can walk around, with a shadow that has a nose in it | one session on top of A | low — cards remain the fallback for anything without a body |
| **B. Full 3D cast** | export persons and sets from Blender to glTF, LOD and bake textures, write a glTF loader + skinning + animation, rebuild every screen | like `ch1-in.png` but lit worse; strand hair becomes cards, clothes lose their folds | days–weeks | high — the look regresses against the stills and the schedule eats the game |
| **C. Prerendered 4K plates** | render each scene in Cycles at 3840×2160, ship layered plates, composite in the browser with parallax and beat-driven camera moves | genuinely 4K, and the best realism the existing models allow | one session per batch + render time, zero GPU at play time | low technically, but it is not interactive 3D |
| **D. Keep 2D, add film response** | tone curve, vignette, grain, subtle DOF and colour grade over the current stage | a better-looking 2D game | hours | none |

None of these makes the game photoreal. The art pack is a painted concept sheet;
everything derived from it inherits illustration. Photoreal humans would need a
scanned-character pipeline the project does not have.

## Recommendation

**A, now.** It is the only reading that changes the *format* of play (real 3D
scene, real camera, real light) without asking for assets that do not exist, and
it is reversible: `?stage=2d` puts the old CSS stage back. **C** is the right
complement for cutscenes and for chapter backdrops once a room is built — same
Blender pipeline, 4K output, no runtime cost. **B** is a different project with
another budget; it should only start if someone is modelling and texturing the
cast.

## Design rules for A

- **Degrades, never breaks.** No WebGL2 → `createStage3D()` returns null and the
  screen renders exactly as it does today. `boot-check` and `smoke` keep running
  headless because nothing touches GL at import.
- **Cast stays billboards — on the beat stage.** A 254×300 atlas crop drawn on a
  camera-facing quad, alpha-tested and shadow-casting, so the close-up keeps its own
  art and the cost stays in one shader. The walkable world is the exception: there
  the cast is rebuilt from volumes, because a stage you stand in is a stage you walk
  around, and a card has no side view (see *The cast in volumes* below).
- **Resolution is a knob:** `?render=1080|1440|4k`, default `min(devicePixelRatio
  × box, 1440)`. 4K is available for screenshots and for machines that carry it.
- **What it will not be:** photoreal, and not a replacement for `ch1-in.png`.

## What is built (variant A)

```
src/systems/gl.js         WebGL2 plumbing: mat4, programs, buffers, depth target
src/systems/stage3d.js    the room, the cast, the sun, the shadow map, the loop
src/ui/screens/play.js    mounts the canvas, keeps `.obj` nodes as hit areas
styles/main.css           `.stage--3d`: canvas fills the box, sprite pixels hidden
tools/stage3d-check.mjs   staging math + the null path, no GPU needed
```

```powershell
npm run stage3d    # staging math, the set, and the "no WebGL2" contract
npm test           # ~1220 steps, now including stage3d, world3d and bodies
```

The stage is on by default. Two switches matter:

| switch | effect |
| --- | --- |
| `?stage=2d` | the old CSS stage, unchanged |
| `?render=1080\|1440\|4k` | render height. An explicit value supersamples; the default is `min(devicePixelRatio × box, 1440)` |

**How the cast is staged.** The chapter data gives each figure `x%`, `y%` and a
size class. The stage casts a ray from the camera through that point: if it meets
the floor, the figure stands there; if it points above the horizon (the sun, the
moon, the answer cards at `y ≈ 40%`), it is pinned to the back wall. Height comes
from the size class times the frustum height at that depth, so a figure keeps the
size it had on the 2D stage — the composition is the same, only the space is real.

**What the beats still drive.** Nothing in `play.js` changed except the mount and
the dispose: the renderer reads the `.obj` nodes' classes every frame
(`is-hidden`, `is-focus`, `is-speaking`, `is-tap`, `is-good`, `is-wrong`) and
mirrors them into glow, tint, bob and visibility. Clicks, tags and the answer
cards all still hit the DOM.

**Measured.** Chrome, Intel UHD Graphics (0x9A68), stage box 876×546:

| render | backing store | frames in 2.2 s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| default | 876×546 (device pixels) | 270 (vsync) |
| `?render=4k` | 3465×2160 | 30 (≈14 fps) |

So 4K is real and honest — it is just not playable on integrated graphics, which
is what the cap is for.

## The walkable world (variant A, second pass)

The beat stage is a close-up: a puppet theatre you watch. The *other* half of the
game — `renderExplore()`, the room you walk around in before a beat starts — was
a painted tile map. That is now a real 3D place as well, and it is where the
chapter-specific geography lives.

```
src/systems/world3d.js     the plan: tiles -> metres, walls, props, camera, light   (pure)
src/systems/bodies3d.js    the cast out of parts: proportions, poses, palette      (pure)
src/systems/meshes.js      five unit solids, uploaded once, reused by every part
src/systems/scene3d.js     the picture: programs, shadow map, draw order, picking
src/ui/screens/explore.js  mounts it; the game logic above it is unchanged
tools/world3d-check.mjs    the plan, the camera and the null path, no GPU needed
tools/bodies-check.mjs     proportions, winding, the poses and the draw path
```

```powershell
npm run world3d   # world3d ok: 5 rooms, 28 props, 429 surfaces, 0.62 m tiles
npm run bodies    # bodies ok: 8 bodies, 201 parts, 1160 triangles of unit mesh
```

**Four modules, four jobs.** `world.js` owns what is solid. `world3d.js` owns what
the room *is*, `bodies3d.js` owns what the cast is built of, and every function in
both is pure, so the whole set can be asserted from Node. `meshes.js` owns only the
five unit solids and their upload. `scene3d.js` owns only the picture.
`explore.js` keeps owning the game: the grid, the keys, the pathfinding, the beats,
the hotspot buttons. The renderer never moves the player; it reads the player's tile
position once a frame through a getter and draws the room around it.

**The map becomes the building.** `room.map` is already 18×12 tiles of `#` (wall),
`.` (floor), `,` (rug), `w` (window), `p` (plant), `m` (mat), `u` (puddle),
`~` (chalk). One tile is 0.62 m, so a room is 11.2 × 7.4 m. Horizontal runs of a
character become one box — an 18-tile wall is one draw call — and a run *breaks*
when the character changes, which is how a window stays an opening instead of
being painted onto a wall. Indoors: plaster walls, a plank floor, glazing with a
mullion and a sill, a rug, a ceiling. Outdoors (`yard`, `evening`): a picket
fence you can see through, grass or asphalt, six sky bands, stars at dusk, and a
ground plane so the horizon has land under it.

**Furniture is boxes; the cast is volumes.** The art pack painted people and
animals, not furniture, so `PROP_GEOMETRY` builds a crib, a dresser, a shelf, a
chalkboard, a table, an easel, a street lamp, a tree — 4 to 8 boxes each, with
materials that match the theme. Figures and animals are built from primitives as
well now (see *The cast in volumes*), and only small props stay billboards. A
hotspot's DOM label is projected from its 3D anchor every frame, so the label, the
tap target and the sprite are one thing.

**The camera is in the head.** The walkable world is first person: the lens sits at
the child's eye height (`EYE_CHILD` 1.34 m, `EYE_BABY` 0.62 m), the arrows turn and
WASD walks relative to the view, and `explore.js` keeps owning yaw and pitch — the
scene only eases towards them. Everything under the lens is unchanged: the walk is
still expressed in tiles and handed to the same `moveEntity()`, so the grid, the
collision and the pathfinder do not move; and in 3D a floor click is a ray cast onto
the floor plane followed by the same `findPath()`. The 2D keys keep their 2D meaning
when the renderer is absent (`KEYMAP_2D` versus `KEYMAP_FPS`). The south wall of
every room is built as a 0.3 m skirt so it can never come between the lens and the
room once the camera turns round.

**What the gameplay drives.** Nothing in the beat logic changed. Every frame the
renderer reads the classes the room already sets on its hotspot buttons —
`is-near`, `is-next`, `is-done`, `is-locked` — and turns them into glow, a pulse
and visibility, the same trick the beat stage plays with `is-focus`.

| switch | effect |
| --- | --- |
| `?world=2d` | the painted tile room, unchanged |
| `?render=1080\|1440\|4k` | render height for both stages |

**Degrades, never breaks.** No WebGL2, no shader, no canvas → `createScene3D()`
returns `null` and `explore.js` paints the 2D room exactly as before. That is not
a fallback path bolted on: the painted layers are simply not built until the
renderer has said no.

**Where the cast is still a drawing.** A billboard in a 1221×813 room is ~200 px
tall, and the painted cast maps are 12×12 letters — a fifth of a head per pixel.
`src/systems/figures.js` rasterizes the cast on a 44×64 cell grid instead (see
docs/sprites.md), so the beat stage draws a figure with a face and a four-frame walk
cycle, and mama, papa, the teacher and the dog are figures there too. The walkable
world builds a body for every name `bodyPlan()` has a kit for, and falls back to the
same drawn figures for anything else. The player keeps a sheet per facing (`front`,
`side`, `back`) for that fallback path only — in first person you see your own body,
not your own card.

Billboards are also stopped down to `0.34` of the room's exposure. The room's own
albedos are dark (`#3b2d5c` arrives at ~0.03 linear) and its figures are pale (a
skin tone arrives at ~0.9), so at the surface stop an unlit face reached three
times the light it needed and clipped to white — the cast looked like paper. The
same constant does the same job on the beat stage.

**Measured.** Chrome, Intel UHD Graphics (0x9A68), room box 1221×813:
390 frames in ~2.4 s (vsync), 0 console errors. Walking 700 ms north moved the
player 1.19 m; a floor click walked 1.5 m to the tile under the cursor.

## The cast in volumes (variant A, third pass)

A card is the right drawing with no thickness: from the side it is a line, and the
shadow it leaves is a card-shaped hole. So the walkable world builds its cast out
of volumes instead, in the card's own palette.

**Five solids, six-units-tall capsule.** A sphere, a dome, a cone, a capsule and
the room's own cube are the whole vocabulary (`meshes.js`). The capsule is
deliberately six units tall with its cap centres at y = ±2, so a limb between two
joints is `[r, len / 6, r]` and its caps land on the joints whatever the limb's
length.

**One part list, two passes.** `bodyPlan()` hands the same parts to the colour pass
and to the shadow pass — each part a mesh, a position, a rotation, a scale and a
material — so the shadow on the floor has a nose and elbows in it and changes when
the body walks. Meshes are rebound only when the mesh changes, not per part.

**Proportions are ratios, not measurements.** One kit of joint ratios builds a
1.7 m papa and a 0.78 m baby, and head size is the ratio that makes a child read as
a child. The palette is `FIGURE_SPECS` verbatim, so the 3D mama is the pink mama the
beats are about.

**First person.** The player's own card is `shadowOnly` and the body skips every
part above eye height minus 5 cm, so the lens sits inside a real head instead of
looking through a drawing of one. The body turns and walks with the input and
plants its feet with the stride rather than floating on it.

**Props turn to face you.** A card has a front; a body does not. Props ease their
yaw towards the camera over about half a second (`BODY_NOTICE`), which keeps a
painted face readable while the room stays real.

**Normals need the inverse transpose.** Every part is a unit solid scaled out of
round — a limb is `[0.05, 0.12, 0.05]` — so `mat3(uModel)` tilts its normals by
about a radian and lights it as if it were a ball. `normalMatrix()` in `gl.js` is
the cofactor matrix over the determinant, which is exactly the inverse transpose,
and `stage3d-check` asserts a face normal stays square to its face through a model
that is both turned and squashed.

**Cloth is keyed to the cloth.** The woven pattern samples each piece's own
coordinates (a unit solid's vertex times the scale in its model), not world space:
keyed to the room, a weave would swim across a body every time it took a step.

**Cards are still the fallback.** A sprite with no `bodyKit` entry, a body whose
plan is null, and every figure on the beat stage still draw as a card — the same
alpha-tested billboard, the same alpha-tested shadow.

**Checked how?** `npm test` proves the numbers: 8 bodies, 201 parts, 1160 triangles
of unit mesh, feet on y = 0, the walk cycle swinging its legs, and a fake-GL section
that asserts the upload/bind/draw/release path part by part. The picture was checked
the same way the room was: headless Chrome with SwiftShader at
`http://localhost:5173`, the room left running for a few seconds, `gl.getError()`
read off the live context — `0` — and the frame captured. It shows the cast standing
on the floor as a body: hair, neck, pink top, arms with hands, trousers, feet, and
no card anywhere.

```powershell
npm run bodies   # bodies ok: 8 bodies, 201 parts, 1160 triangles of unit mesh
```

## What this pass does not do

- **The beat stage still uses one room for five backdrops.** The walkable world
  is chapter-specific; the close-up is not. `roomPlan()` changes colours, light
  and one prop (the chalkboard) per backdrop, not the layout, so chapter 2's
  dialogue still happens in a room-shaped space. It is a deliberate stage
  conceit rather than a bug, but it is the biggest remaining lie.
- **The beat stage still draws its cast as cards.** The walkable world builds
  bodies; the close-up does not, because a beat is watched head-on and a card at
  0.34 exposure is a better face than anything five primitives can fake.
- **Bodies are primitives, not characters.** A head, a neck, a chest, a pelvis, two
  arms, two legs, ears, a tail: no skin, no folds, no fabric. They read as a person
  the way a mannequin does. AAA fidelity is the glTF-and-skinning project (variant
  B), not something a part list can grow into.
- **One shadow map, no AO.** A single 2048² depth map with 3×3 PCF. No contact
  shadows, no bounce, no bloom. Ceilings and skies are excluded from the shadow
  pass on purpose: a ceiling is not a roof, and shadowing the whole room with one
  is how a 3D interior turns black.
- **Lighted walls read as a row of panels.** Side walls are built one tile at a
  time — `wallPlan()` merges runs horizontally, and a 1-tile-wide column has no
  neighbour to merge with — so every slab's edge drops a thin shadow on the next
  one and the cap between two lit slabs is visible as a vertical line. Merging
  vertical runs (or a slope-scaled bias in `shadowAt`) is the fix; it belongs with
  the lighting pass, not with the cast.
- **Nothing here has watched the bodies move.** The static frame is verified; the
  cloth weave that is now keyed to each piece and the shadow that has to follow a
  walking body were checked as numbers (the shader source and the matrix math), not
  as motion. That needs a human at `npm start`.
- **The camera does not orbit.** Fixed yaw, follow position. Rotating the camera
  means rotating the movement basis too, which is a gameplay decision, not a
  rendering one, and the 2D game's controls had to survive the change unharmed.
- **Sprites are unlit cards.** They receive the sun, fill and lamp as a whole;
  they do not self-shadow or catch light from each other.
